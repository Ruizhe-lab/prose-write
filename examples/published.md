# Examples: short quotes from published papers, by function

Read the function you are about to write. Note one or two construction choices and decide whether they transfer. Never copy a sentence or a claim.
Each quote was checked word for word against a copy of the paper (line-break hyphens joined). This repository does not include the papers; obtain them to read further. A new quote enters only after the same check, with its page.

Sources:
- DGP: Duffie, Gârleanu and Pedersen (2005), "Over-the-Counter Markets", *Econometrica* 73(6).
- DZ: Duffie and Zhu (2011), "Does a Central Clearing Counterparty Reduce Counterparty Risk?", *Review of Asset Pricing Studies* 1(1).
- Zhu: Zhu (2014), "Do Dark Pools Harm Price Discovery?", *Review of Financial Studies* 27(3).
- BCS: Budish, Cramton and Shim (2015), "The High-Frequency Trading Arms Race", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 130(4).
- BJZZ: Boehmer, Jones, Zhang and Zhang (2021), "Tracking Retail Investor Activity", *Journal of Finance* 76(5). Pages are those of the accepted manuscript.
- BLLO: Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean (2009), "Just How Much Do Individual Investors Lose by Trading?", *Review of Financial Studies* 22(2). Printed pages.

## Abstract: question, then findings, no model tour

DGP, p. 1815: "We study how intermediation and asset prices in over-the-counter markets are affected by illiquidity associated with search and bargaining. ... Bid–ask spreads are lower if investors can more easily find other investors or have easier access to multiple marketmakers."

## Introduction, first paragraph: the friction in the market's own terms

DGP, p. 1815: "IN OVER-THE-COUNTER MARKETS, an investor who wishes to sell must search for a buyer, incurring opportunity or other costs until one is found."
Zhu, p. 747: "Dark pools are equity trading systems that do not publicly display orders."

## The thesis stated on the first pages

BCS, p. 1549: "We argue that the high-frequency trading arms race is a symptom of a basic flaw in the design of modern financial exchanges: continuous-time trading."

## Stakes: one measured fact against a yardstick the reader owns

BCS, p. 1548: "Round-trip communication time between New York and Chicago was reduced . . . from 16 milliseconds to 13 milliseconds. ... (The blink of a human eye lasts 400 milliseconds".

## Mechanism taught with one worked number

DZ, p. 76: "Suppose that Dealer A is exposed to Dealer B by $100 million on CDS, while at the same time Dealer B is exposed to Dealer A by $150 million on interest-rate swaps. The bilateral exposure is the net, $50 million."

## A magnitude made felt by a threshold

DZ, p. 76: "It is far from obvious that clearable exposures in the CDS market are nearly this large."
The sentence before it states the threshold (66%); this one tells the reader how to feel about it.

## "Surprising" only after the benchmark, with the reason at once

DGP, p. 1825: "It might seem surprising that a single monopolistic marketmaker is equivalent for pricing purposes to many "competing" nonatomic marketmakers. The result follows from the fact that a search economy is inherently uncompetitive".

## Discussion of a comparative static: one object, its reason

DGP, p. 1825: "The bid–ask spread is decreasing in λ, since a high λ means that an investor can easily find a counterparty himself, which improves his bargaining position."

## A modelling choice: what it matches, what it isolates

- Practice match. Zhu, p. 754: "this midpoint execution method is common in dark pools operated by agency brokers and exchanges."
- What a strong assumption lets the paper show. BCS, pp. 1576-77: "This is a best-case scenario for price discovery and liquidity provision in a continuous limit order book, abstracting from both asymmetric information and inventory costs."
- A fixed choice with its practical reason. DGP, p. 1831: "This assumption is convenient and can be motivated by interpreting the search intensity as based on a technology that is difficult to change."

## Related literature

- Opener that makes a claim about the literature. BJZZ, p. 3: "Many researchers have concluded that retail equity investors are generally uninformed". BLLO, p. 610, footnote markers dropped: "Several prior studies provide evidence that individual investors lose from trade, while institutions profit."
- The gap in mid-paragraph. BJZZ, p. 3: "Unfortunately, most existing studies of retail order flow are based on proprietary datasets with relatively small subsets of overall retail order flow."
- The closest rival, with the exact divergence. BJZZ, p. 6: "We are able to replicate the predictive relation between retail order flow and future stock returns. However, our results for the contemporaneous relation are different".
- A contribution paragraph. BJZZ, p. 5: "we make three main contributions. First and most importantly, we propose a novel methodology for identifying and signing marketable retail trades using publicly available data".

## Conclusion

- A short conclusion: result, policy implication, implementation constraint. DZ, p. 90, its first sentence: "We show that the separate central clearing of one class of derivatives, such as credit default swaps, could reduce netting efficiency, leading to higher expected counterparty exposures and collateral demands." The rest of the paragraph gives one policy recommendation and the practical hurdles to it.
- No separate conclusion. DGP has none: its last section is "8. Empirical Implications", then the appendix. Leaving one out is a real option.
- A conclusion that names the belief it overturns. Zhu, p. 779: "The results of this paper challenge the conventional wisdom that dark trading is by definition harmful for price discovery."
